# What the 2024–25 figures say

These notes use the pinned AIHW MyHospitals tables (accessed 8 November 2026) and, where a patient-level national figure cannot be rebuilt from hospital rows, the Institute’s own published summary. People counted here had already been admitted. This is not the list of people still waiting.

## National picture

- The Institute reports that half of public elective patients were admitted within **45 days** in 2024–25, down from **46 days** in 2023–24. Nine in ten were admitted within **329 days**.
- **6.0%** waited more than 365 days, down from **6.4%** in 2023–24 and **7.6%** in 2020–21. About **791,000** people were admitted from public waiting lists.
- The national specialty table in this project sums to **790,528** operations in 2024–25. The surgery-weighted share waiting more than a year is **5.9%** (0.05936). That is the same story as the published 6.0%, on a file that is a few hundred admissions smaller.
- In these tables the share waiting more than a year was **2.8%** in 2019–20, **7.6%** in 2020–21, **9.6%** in 2022–23, **6.4%** in 2023–24, and **5.9%** in 2024–25.
- A surgery-weighted average of the national specialty waits is **52.9 days**. That is not the 45-day figure. Averaging specialty waits, even when busier specialties count for more, is not the same as lining up every patient and taking the middle one.

## Where the recommended time is missed

Recommended times used here are the usual clinical categories: 30 days for urgent, 90 days for semi-urgent, 365 days for non-urgent. Hospital shares below are surgery-weighted across public hospitals with a published share. Suppressed cells are left out of the share.

| Urgency | Admissions with a published share, roughly | Treated within the recommended time | People outside that time | Hospitals where the typical wait is already past the limit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Urgent (30 days) | 255,100 | 90.7% | about 23,600 | 3 |
| Semi-urgent (90 days) | 307,900 | 72.0% | about 86,150 | 16 |
| Non-urgent (365 days) | 225,700 | 81.2% | about 42,400 | 10 |

Semi-urgent surgery is the volume problem. Urgent surgery is mostly on time nationally, but it has slipped: **95.7%** of urgent operations were inside 30 days in 2019–20, and **90.7%** were in 2024–25. Semi-urgent on-time performance fell to **65.2%** in 2022–23 and has recovered only to **72.0%**, still short of **83.0%** in 2019–20.

The ACT specialty rate is extreme on a smaller list.

## Hospitals and peer groups

Hospitals where the typical urgent wait is already past 30 days: Rockingham General Hospital (WA, 40 days, 1,042 operations), Carnarvon Hospital (WA, 34 days, 56 operations), Charleville Hospital (Qld, 33 days, 38 operations). Rockingham is the one with real volume.

The larger counts of people treated outside the recommended time are not always those three. In 2024–25 the biggest gaps rebuilt from the published share are:

- Sydney Hospital / Sydney Eye Hospital, non-urgent: typical wait **378 days** (past the 365-day mark), about **2,365** people outside the recommended time, only **35%** inside it.
- Royal Adelaide Hospital, urgent: typical wait **26 days**, still inside 30, but only **60%** were. About **2,218** urgent patients were not.
- Royal Adelaide Hospital, semi-urgent: typical wait **107 days**, about **2,084** people outside 90 days.
- Royal Children’s Hospital [Parkville], semi-urgent: typical wait **102 days**, about **2,026** people.
- Royal Brisbane & Women’s Hospital, semi-urgent: typical wait **92 days**, about **2,022** people.
- The Canberra Hospital, semi-urgent: typical wait **114 days**, **39%** inside 90 days, about **1,814** people.

Children’s hospitals (**77.6%** on time) and large regional hospitals (**77.9%**) are the peer groups furthest from the recommended time. Small hospitals are higher, at **86.1%**, which is a different mix of work rather than a reason to ignore them.

## Operations

National published waits in 2024–25, and the change since 2023–24 where it matters:

| Operation | Admissions | Half were admitted within | Waited over a year | Previous year |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Septoplasty | 5,687 | 320 days | 30.8% | 332 days, 37.9% |
| Total knee replacement | 17,987 | 255 days | 23.2% | 265 days, 25.7% |
| Tonsillectomy | 16,182 | 182 days | 17.0% | 185 days, 18.4% |
| Total hip replacement | 12,180 | 130 days | 13.0% | 135 days, 15.7% |
| Cataract extraction | 87,841 | 106 days | 11.5% | 119 days, 6.7% |

Cataract surgery is the awkward one. The typical wait improved by 13 days, and the Institute reports the same 119-to-106-day move, but the share waiting more than a year rose from **6.7%** to **11.5%**. A shorter middle of the list can hide a thicker tail.

Among specialties nationally, half of ear, nose and throat patients were admitted within **99 days** (**14.4%** waited over a year; 60,031 operations). Ophthalmology was **92 days** and **10.6%** (117,596 operations). Orthopaedics was **86 days** and **10.8%** (103,750 operations). Cardio-thoracic surgery was the shortest typical wait, **20 days**.

## Where attention should go first

1. Urgent surgery in Tasmania, then the Northern Territory and the ACT. Victoria and New South Wales show that the 30-day mark is still being met at state scale.
2. Semi-urgent capacity in the ACT, Tasmania, South Australia, and the Northern Territory, with Canberra Hospital, Royal Adelaide, the Royal Children’s in Parkville, and the Royal Brisbane & Women’s Hospital on the first hospital list.
3. Long waits over a year in the ACT and New South Wales, especially NSW eye surgery, knee replacement, septoplasty, and the cataract tail.
4. Do not manage only to the typical wait. Royal Adelaide’s urgent list is the example: the middle of the list is inside 30 days, and about 2,200 people are not.

## What this file does not claim

- It does not rebuild the 45-day national figure from hospital medians.
- It does not report an “average overdue wait” in days. That AIHW indicator is a different table, and the Institute warns it is not comparable across states because it depends on how urgency is assigned.
- Northern Territory records have an AIHW quality note about possible duplication. Treat the NT trend with more caution than the larger states.
- Counts of people outside the recommended time are rebuilt from a published percentage and an admission count, so they can be off by a small amount where the percentage was rounded.
